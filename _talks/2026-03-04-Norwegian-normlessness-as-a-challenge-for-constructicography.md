With Anna Endresen, Paulina Horbowicz, Jakub Przytuła, Julia Jaworska and Andrea Romanzi

Norwegian has two official written standards: Bokmål (lit. ‘book language’, a Danish-influenced variety that accounts for over 80% of published production in Norway) and Nynorsk (lit. ‘new Norwegian’, a dialect-based alternative devised by grammarian Ivar Aasen in the 19th century (as detailed in Vikør 2022; Faarlund et al. 1997: 2; Holmes & Enger 2018: 4-5). The two written standards are traditionally treated as varieties of the same language but argued to diverge substantially in inflectional morphology (Åfarli, Vulchanova, Lohndal 2013) and idiomatic usage (Vikør 2009). Phraseological comparisons suggest that differences are often matters of degree, with many expressions usable in both norms yet more strongly associated with one (Rauset 2022). For example, Bokmål favors the construction litt av en X (as in litt av en fyr), whereas Nynorsk uses the construction noko til X (as in noko til kar), both roughly corresponding to ‘quite an X’ in English (in our examples: quite a guy).

All existing constructicon resources model only a single prescriptive written standard, whereas Norwegian requires a multi-norm approach (Endresen & Mikkelsen 2024) and thus poses a challenge for constructicography. We explore how the Norwegian constructicon project (Språknett 2024) should represent constructions in a language with no single written norm. More specifically, do Bokmål and Nynorsk exhibit constructional differences that cannot be reduced to vocabulary choice or inflectional morphology, warranting distinct constructicographic treatment?

We use two literary translations of Tolkien’s The Hobbit (1937) as our primary comparative corpus for Bokmål (Agøy 1972) and Nynorsk (Myhren 2008). These parallel texts serve as a controlled setting for identifying divergences beyond lexicon and morphology.

Based on this data, we address the following questions: How are the two varieties best represented in a single constructicon resource for the Norwegian language? Should the Bokmål and Nynorsk constructions have distinct entries or a single entry, given the existing correspondences and divergences between the two standard varieties? In this talk, we report our empirical findings and discuss their implications for the architecture of the interface of the Norwegian Constructicon.
